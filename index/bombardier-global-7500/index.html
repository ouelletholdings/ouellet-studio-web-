Why it's the reference airframe

The 7500 is the aircraft most likely to anchor a heavy-jet monograph commission: it's the current top of the purpose-built (not airliner-derivative) business jet market, which means owners and operators treat it as a flagship asset worth documenting properly rather than just logging for a listing. Four discrete cabin zones — typically a stateroom-style suite, a conference/dining area, a lounge, and a separate crew rest — give a shoot real compositional variety inside one aircraft, which a smaller cabin jet can't offer.

Exterior angles that work

Low 3/4 from the nose captures the fuselage taper and the Pearl 15 nacelles in one frame without the wingtip winglets competing for attention. A true nose-on composition emphasizes the aircraft's width and the symmetry of the twin-engine mount — strong for a hero frame, weak as a context shot since it hides the fuselage length that makes this airframe read as "large" against a ramp.

Interior light

Cabin glazing on the 7500 is generous relative to most business jets, which helps and hurts: good ambient fill during daylight ramp shoots, but mixed color temperature the moment cabin tungsten or LED accent lighting is on alongside daylight through the windows. Per the studio's standard workflow, that gets corrected at the color-calibration stage before any creative grade — never shot around by closing shades, which kills the depth the glazing is there to provide.

Automatic reject, per the studio standard: any frame showing a visible tail number or cabin identifying mark without logged owner/operator approval. The 7500's size makes the registration block on the aft fuselage prominent in most 3/4 exterior compositions — plan the crop before the shoot, not after.
